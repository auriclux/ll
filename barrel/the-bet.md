# The Bet

Start with the room where it will probably not happen.

My favorite tap room sits in the marina, fifty selections and more, and on the right evening the sun goes down over the water in a way that makes a man forgive the whole day. My nephew John is beside me. A machine brings us a beer that nobody paid for, in any sense that would have made sense five years earlier, and the bet is over.

That room will be among the last on Earth to adopt physical AI. It is not a tech-forward place. That is part of its charm and most of its business model. So the bet will likely settle somewhere else: a tech-forward room in a good setting, near Torrance by default, or Burbank, or the Bay Area, or anywhere on the planet the two of us can arrange to sit side by side. Kyoto would do. Johannesburg would do. The bet travels with us, because the bet was never about the room. The room is just where I see it most clearly.

## What the bet is

John and I argue about the Autonimity. That is my name for the shift Elon Musk points at when he says money will stop mattering: automation eats the labor share of products and services, AI and free energy do the eating, cost falls, price follows cost down, and in the limit the common things of life, the high-production, high-use things, are simply free, with human labor removed from them. Specialty items still take money. They become the exceptions that prove the rule.

The bet is a recognition test for the boundary of that world. It says: on or before New Year's Eve 2030, the two of us, sitting together, will be able to order a free beer, delivered by a robot, physical AI, in a process with no service human in the loop and no money in the transaction. If that happens, I win. If December 31, 2030 closes without it, John wins, and I buy the beer. With money.

Note the modesty of the test, because the modesty is the point. Musk's date is 2036. I am not betting that money ends by 2030, or that the Autonimity arrives whole. I only have to tip the ball into the basket. The boundary between before and now is a range, not a line, and the bet resolves at the start of the range: the first honest instance. One common process, somewhere we can reach it, with the labor share driven to negligible and the human removed from it in kind. A toe over the line is a crossing.

## How it started

I had been toying with the Autonimity a little before Elon said it out loud. We must have been reading the same articles. Then, on July 23, 2026, in an interview with The Economist at the Texas Gigafactory, he declared that money would not matter by 2036, and the idea had a headline and a date. That story is what started the discussions with John. We argued the what and the when for a while, the way we argue everything, in the academic sense, and somewhere in that argument we got around to the free beer as a test of the process. A beer is small, common, and ancient. If the future cannot be detected in a beer, it is not much of a future.

John's position has two legs. First, the technology cannot advance that quickly. Second, even if it did, people would not accept it. He makes this argument from inside one of the hyperscalers, where he works, so it carries the weight of a man doubting the pace of the very building he stands in. I challenge the first leg and I grant the second its merit, with one caveat: if a thing benefits the individual without challenging his ideology, it gets used, and then it gets normalized, and then nobody remembers objecting to it. Civilization rarely votes the future in. It carries the future home in its pocket because the battery life was better.

## How we will know

We are not going to lawyer this. The rules below exist so that the moment, when it comes, recognizes itself.

We must be together, physically, in the same room or at the same table. That was the underlying assumption from the start, so obvious we never said it until someone asked. The beer must be orderable by us, in the ordinary way a customer orders, and the process from order through delivery must be automated, with AI in the path. Any service human in the loop breaks it: a human who pours, carries, or opens the fridge has put the labor back, and the labor is the whole question. A human supervisor does not break it. The early autonomous cars carried safety drivers and were autonomous anyway; a supervisor watching a process is not a pair of hands inside it.

The shape of the machine is its own business. A humanoid would be satisfying. A drone that drops a can on our table counts, provided no human opened the fridge for it, and it charms me more than the obvious candidate, the wheeled tray robot, which counts on a technicality and would be grandly disappointing, especially behind a human pour. A physical AI in the form of a multitentacled slug, delivering the glasses, would be well within the intent of the bet. But, ew.

The beer must be free, and free has a strict meaning here. It is not a gift, not a comp, not a promotion. It is free because that is where the process has progressed. There are no shell games with money: no bundling the beer into food, no cover charge wearing a disguise, no obligation of any kind attached. We have to be able to walk in, have our free beer, and owe nothing. The selection may be limited. The house may ration the number of free beers, and will, if it makes its money on the food, which it may, because the pizza is not free: a human still has to cook it. The Autonimity will arrive process by process, not all at once, and a free beer beside a priced pizza is not a contradiction. It is a photograph of the boundary, mid-crossing.

And the cheat, the one we agree on without reservation: no staged, subsidized, or teleoperated demonstrations. A launch event with a robot prop and a marketing budget is the future posing for a photograph, and it settles nothing. The exception proves the rule's intent: a trade show that demonstrates a real, scalable process of serving a drink, a process that belongs to the labor-free end state, is a toe over the line, and a toe is all I have ever claimed.

## The earlier bets

This is the third of these I can still put my hands on, and the first one getting a written record while it is young. The others show why records matter.

In late summer or fall of 2016, at a wedding banquet, with Trump running against Clinton, I bemoaned the obvious advantage to Trump. John was certain the country would not elect so low a character. He was betting on the electorate's character; I was betting, regretfully, on the machinery underneath it. The stakes, as with most of ours, were just the righteous glory of being correct. I have been dining out on that one for a decade, which tells you what the actual currency between us is.

The autonomous cars bet is half lost, and I say so here because a ledger that hides its gaps is not a ledger. I remember the shape of it: the cars were a test-track novelty, there had been at least one run on city streets with a safety driver, and the question was how soon they would be on our streets in earnest. The proposition has evaporated. What survives is its standard, the safety driver rule, which now adjudicates the beer. The precedents outlive their paperwork. That is either comforting or alarming, depending on the precedent.

The pattern across all three is the same. John bets on how things ought to hold. I bet on what the machinery is doing. For once, with the Autonimity, the machinery has me on the optimistic side, and I confess I enjoy the novelty.

## The sparring partner

People ask, in effect, why John. There was no selection process. He is my nephew, which disposes of the scheduling problem across four years and any number of time zones: in four years, he'll still be my nephew. The real qualification is simpler and rarer. He is the only person I know whose eyes do not roll back when the obscure subjects come out, neither from bored incomprehension nor from the headache that incomprehension induces. He engages intelligently. And he suffers a similar intellectual loneliness among his own crowd, as I do among mine. Every man with a head full of strange weather needs one other person who will stand in it with him and argue. John is mine. Sparring partner is the term. Not opponent. An opponent you try to defeat. A sparring partner you try to still be standing with, four years on, when the bell rings.

## The stakes, honestly

If I win, John buys me a ticket to Asia, destination to be determined, likely Japan, likely Kyoto. If I lose, I buy the beer, with money, a sentence that writes its own punchline if the Autonimity has anything to say about it.

Two honest footnotes. The ticket is a specialty item in a still-expensive category; it would cost real money, and I would not want it fulfilled at that cost. The prize was always partly theater. And the theater was always partly the point, because the true stake in every bet John and I have ever made is the one named at that wedding banquet: the righteous glory of being correct. Money is what the bet is about. It was never what the bet was for.

## The standing appointment

On the morning of November 8, 2026, I sent John a link to a website I had just built, a place to keep the leaves of all this thinking. He wrote back that we could use it to keep us accountable on the bets we make. This page is that accountability, written down while the bet is still young enough to remember itself.

It stays open until New Year's Eve 2030. Then it gets its second half, written by events: either a short entry about a beer that cost nothing, in a room I cannot yet name, or a short entry about a beer I bought, with money, in a world that had not finished changing. Either way, John will be sitting next to me when it is written. That part was never in doubt. It was the assumption underneath everything else.
